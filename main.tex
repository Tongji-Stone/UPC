{\Large\bfseries An Aerodynamic and Thermodynamic Analysis\\[3pt] of the Maximum Release Altitude for Space Diving}\\[10pt]
{\groupfont 作业小组：骆欣畅\quad 魏贤东\quad 刘奕韬}\\[12pt]
\end{center}

\begin{abstract}
For the 190\,kg space-diving system consisting of the diver, pressure suit, and parachute, this work develops an integrated descent model within a layered atmospheric and vertical-motion framework to investigate the release altitude. The descent process is divided into three stages: free fall, finite-time parachute inflation, and fully deployed canopy descent and landing. During free fall, gravitational and aerodynamic forces are modeled using an altitude-dependent atmospheric model and an empirical Mach-dependent drag coefficient. Aerodynamic heating is evaluated through the recovery-temperature formulation, and a local flat-spin response model is introduced to analyze short-time rotational hazards. During parachute deployment and subsequent descent, the finite-time inflation process, opening loads, and landing dynamics are established.

The model is calibrated using the measured maximum-speed data from the 2012 Red Bull Stratos space-diving missions. The effective frontal area of the system is jointly determined from the peak velocities of these missions. The simulated speed--altitude trajectory is then compared with the measured trajectory of the main mission in October 2012 to validate the model.

The results reveal three important characteristics: transonic drag rise dominates the aerodynamic load increase during high-speed descent; low-altitude terminal-speed convergence determines the final descent state after parachute deployment; and aerodynamic heating provides the primary limitation on the maximum release altitude. Under the constraints that the recovery temperature does not exceed \HeatLimit\,K, the free-fall aerodynamic load and head radial acceleration remain below $\FreeLimit\,g_0$ and $\SpinLimit\,g_0$, respectively, the parachute-opening load remains below $\OpenLimit\,g_0$, and the landing velocity remains below \LandingLimit\,m/s, the maximum allowable release altitude is \MaxHeight\,km. At this altitude, the peak velocity reaches \PeakV\,m/s, with aerodynamic heating as the governing constraint. As a comparison, considering only the thermal constraint and the free-fall aerodynamic-load constraint, the corresponding maximum release altitudes are \MaxHeight\,km and \LoadHeight\,km, respectively.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} space diving; joint calibration; transonic drag; recovery temperature; attitude dynamics; finite-time inflation

\section{Introduction}
\subsection{Background}
The rapid development of crewed spaceflight, suborbital flight, and commercial space tourism has increased interest in emergency escape and life support in near space. Space diving has also evolved from an extreme sport into a platform for studying human physiology at altitude and supersonic aerodynamics.

In 2012, Felix Baumgartner completed two test space dives and a final mission as part of Red Bull Stratos. The mission summary report~\cite{summit} documents altitude, speed, attitude, and parachute-opening records for all three flights, providing measurements with which to study descent from high altitude. Investigating the descent characteristics of a space-diving system with a specified mass therefore has clear engineering relevance.

\subsection{Problem and Approach}
We consider a rocket carrying a space diver vertically to a release altitude. The diver then exits wearing a pressure suit and carrying a parachute, with a total system mass of 190\,kg, and descends to the surface. Our objective is to estimate the highest altitude from which this descent can be completed while preserving the diver\textquotesingle s safety.

The aerodynamic study~\cite{guerster} uses a belly-down posture, with the body\textquotesingle s longitudinal axis approximately horizontal, as the reference attitude for space diving. We assume that this posture is maintained throughout the descent and divide the process into three stages:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Free fall.} Between release and main-canopy inflation, gravity and aerodynamic drag, which varies with altitude and speed, govern translation; the dynamics are developed in Section~\ref{sec:forces}. At the same time, the deceleration of near-wall airflow raises the recovery temperature, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:heat}. Aerodynamic asymmetry can also induce attitude motion, which is considered in Section~\ref{sec:attitude}.
\item \textbf{Parachute inflation.} The effective canopy area grows over a finite interval, and the resulting drag rapidly decelerates the system. This stage is modeled in Section~\ref{sec:inflation}.
\item \textbf{Descent under the fully inflated canopy and landing.} Body and canopy drag act together as the speed approaches local equilibrium until the altitude reaches zero; Section~\ref{sec:landingmodel} describes this stage.
\end{enumerate}

Section~4 develops the physical models, Section~5 calibrates and examines them using publicly available Red Bull Stratos measurements, and Section~6 evaluates the hazards to determine the maximum admissible release altitude.
\section{Assumptions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The atmosphere is stationary and varies only with altitude. Motion is approximately vertical, the initial vertical speed is zero, and total mass is constant. Horizontal drift, lift, Earth\textquotesingle s rotation, and the rocket wake are neglected.
\item The ground is at sea level, so parachute-opening and landing altitudes share the same geometric reference.
\item The pressure suit remains sealed, with sufficient oxygen and pressure-maintenance capability for the entire computed descent. The parachute is intact and functional.
\item The space diver maintains the belly-down posture throughout the descent.
\item The inertia of the body and equipment is approximated by that of a uniform cylinder. The belly-down reference attitude is assumed to be stable in pitch, with small initial pitch disturbances and zero initial flat-spin angular velocity.
\item The space diver is well trained, can assess the descent state, opens the parachute promptly at the prescribed altitude, and reacts and adjusts posture within 1\,s after detecting flat spin.
\item A space dive is considered successful if all loads experienced during descent remain within human tolerance and body temperature remains normal.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Physical Models}
\subsection{Atmosphere}\label{sec:atmos}
We construct a layered atmosphere from the standard-atmosphere reference~\cite[Table~4]{ussa}. Because its temperature layers are specified in geopotential altitude, geometric altitude must first be converted. Geopotential altitude represents the equivalent height under sea-level gravity for the same change in gravitational potential energy:
\begin{equation}
 z=\frac{R_eh}{R_e+h}.
 \label{eq:height}
\end{equation}
Here $h$ is geometric altitude and $R_e=6.357\times10^3$\,km is the reference Earth radius. The corresponding gravitational acceleration is
\begin{equation}
 g(h)=g_0\left(\frac{R_e}{R_e+h}\right)^2.
 \label{eq:gravity}
\end{equation}
With $g_0=9.807$\,m/s$^2$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:height} ensures that the potential-energy increment satisfies $g(h)\,\mathrm dh=g_0\,\mathrm dz$.

The seven temperature gradients in Table~4 of the standard-atmosphere reference lead to
\begin{equation}
T[\text{K}](z[\text{km}])=\begin{cases}
288.15-6.5z,&0\le z<11,\\
216.65,&11\le z<20,\\
216.65+(z-20),&20\le z<32,\\
228.65+2.8(z-32),&32\le z<47,\\
270.65,&47\le z<51,\\
270.65-2.8(z-51),&51\le z<71,\\
214.65-2(z-71),&71\le z\le84.852.
\end{cases}\label{eq:temperature}
\end{equation}
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:temperature}, atmospheric temperature varies between layers; this structure also determines the altitude dependence of the speed of sound.
\paperfig{atmosphere_temperature}{Temperature versus geometric altitude in the seven-layer standard atmosphere.}{fig:temperature}

The specific gas constant for air is taken as $R=287.1$\,J/(kg K). Hydrostatic equilibrium requires the pressure gradient to balance the weight of the air:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\mathrm dp}{\mathrm dh}=-\rho g(h).
 \label{eq:hydro}
\end{equation}
The ideal-gas law is
\begin{equation}
 p=\rho RT.
 \label{eq:ideal}
\end{equation}
Combining Eqs.~\eqref{eq:height}, \eqref{eq:temperature}, \eqref{eq:hydro}, and \eqref{eq:ideal}, starting from a sea-level pressure of 101.3\,kPa and enforcing pressure continuity across layer interfaces, yields the density profile:
\begin{equation}
 \rho[\text{kg/m}^3](z[\text{km}])=\begin{cases}
 1.225\left(1-\dfrac{6.5z}{288.15}\right)^{4.256},&0\le z<11,\\[3pt]
 0.3639\,e^{-0.1577(z-11)},&11\le z<20,\\[3pt]
 8.803\times10^{-2}\left(1+\dfrac{z-20}{216.65}\right)^{-35.16},&20\le z<32,\\[3pt]
 1.323\times10^{-2}\left(1+\dfrac{2.8(z-32)}{228.65}\right)^{-13.20},&32\le z<47,\\[3pt]
 1.428\times10^{-3}\,e^{-0.1262(z-47)},&47\le z<51,\\[3pt]
 8.616\times10^{-4}\left(1-\dfrac{2.8(z-51)}{270.65}\right)^{11.20},&51\le z<71,\\[3pt]
 6.421\times10^{-5}\left(1-\dfrac{2(z-71)}{214.65}\right)^{16.08},&71\le z\le84.852.
 \end{cases}\label{eq:density}
\end{equation}
Converting back to geometric altitude through Eq.~\eqref{eq:height} produces the density profile in Figure~\ref{fig:density}. Density decreases by several orders of magnitude with increasing altitude, which is the main environmental reason for the contrast between high-altitude acceleration and low-altitude deceleration.
\paperfig{atmosphere_density}{Standard-atmosphere density versus geometric altitude.}{fig:density}

\subsection{Free Fall}
\subsubsection{Forces and Vertical Motion}\label{sec:forces}
As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:forces}, two external forces act vertically during free fall. We represent the system\textquotesingle s translation by the motion of its center of mass.
\par
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[>=Stealth,thick,font=\figurefont\fontsize{11bp}{13bp}\selectfont]

 \draw[->] (1.25,.8)--(1.25,-.9) node[midway,right] {\figvar{v}};
 \draw[->] (-2.4,-.9)--(-2.4,.8) node[midway,left] {Positive \figvar{h}};
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{Forces at the center of mass and the direction of motion during free fall.}\label{fig:forces}
\end{figure}
\par
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Gravity.} The weight $mg(h)$ acts downward, with $g(h)$ defined by Eq.~\eqref{eq:gravity} and total mass $m=190$\,kg.
\item \textbf{Aerodynamic drag.} The large characteristic dimensions of the body and canopy, together with the high descent speed, place the flow in an inertia-dominated continuum regime at high Reynolds number. Aerodynamic forces therefore scale with dynamic pressure, motivating the quadratic drag law
\begin{equation}
 D(h)=\frac12\rho(h)C_D(M)A v(h)^2.
 \label{eq:force}
\end{equation}
Drag acts upward and represents the combined effects of pressure and skin-friction drag. The three aerodynamic parameters are specified as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\renewcommand{\labelenumii}{\arabic{enumii}.}
\item \textbf{Air density.} The density $\rho(h)$ follows Eq.~\eqref{eq:density} and the geopotential-altitude transformation, using the source described in Section~\ref{sec:atmos}.
\item \textbf{Drag coefficient.} As speed approaches the local speed of sound, compressibility becomes significant, and local shocks and changes in pressure distribution increase drag. Thus $C_D$ depends on the ratio of speed to sound speed, namely the Mach number. The local speed of sound is
\[
 c(h)=\sqrt{\gamma RT(h)}.
\]
The Mach number is defined as
\begin{equation*}
 M=\frac{v}{c(h)}.
\end{equation*}
Here $T(h)$ follows Eq.~\eqref{eq:temperature}, and $\gamma=1.4$ is the specific-heat ratio for air under the constant-specific-heat approximation. We adopt the drag relation inferred from measured flight data in the aerodynamic study~\cite{guerster}:
\begin{equation}
 C_D(M)=\begin{cases}
 0.600,&0\le M\le0.6,\\
 0.600+0.550(M-0.6)^2,&0.6<M\le1.1,\\
 0.740-0.320(M-1.1),&1.1<M\le1.25,\\
 0.692,&M>1.25.
 \end{cases}\label{eq:drag}
\end{equation}
The final branch is a constant extrapolation beyond the original data range. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:drag}, $C_D$ rises and then falls across the transonic region.
\item \textbf{Frontal area.} The constant $A$ is the effective aerodynamic area of the body and equipment. It is jointly calibrated against three peak-speed observations in Section~\ref{sec:calibration}.
\end{enumerate}

\end{enumerate}

\paperfig{process_q1_drag}{Adopted $C_D$--$M$ relation; shading marks the constant extrapolation beyond the original data range.}{fig:drag}

With altitude positive upward, Newton\textquotesingle s second law reads
\begin{equation*}
 m\frac{\mathrm d^2h}{\mathrm dt^2}=-mg(h)+D.
\end{equation*}
The release conditions are $h(0)=h_0$ and $\dot h(0)=0$. Defining downward speed as $v=-\dot h$ and applying the chain rule along the descent trajectory gives
\begin{equation}
 v\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dh}=-g(h)+\frac{\rho(h)C_D(M)A}{2m}v^2.
 \label{eq:vh}
\end{equation}
Substitution of $v(h)$ into Eq.~\eqref{eq:force} then determines the drag profile $D(h)$.

\subsubsection{Aerodynamic Heating}\label{sec:heat}
As the body moves rapidly relative to the air, the flow on its windward side decelerates, converting bulk kinetic energy into enthalpy. Near-wall viscous dissipation also raises the temperature. By the first law of thermodynamics, adiabatic deceleration to rest satisfies
\[
 c_p(T_0-T)=\frac{v^2}{2}.
\]
Here $T_0$ is the stagnation temperature. The constant-pressure specific heat $c_p$ is the energy required to raise the temperature of unit mass of air by one degree at constant pressure; $c_v$ in the specific-heat ratio $\gamma=c_p/c_v$ is the constant-volume specific heat. For an ideal gas with constant properties,
\[
 c_p=\frac{\gamma R}{\gamma-1}.
\]
With $\gamma=1.4$ and the gas constant $R$ defined above, $c_p=1.005$\,kJ/(kg K). Viscous transport and thermal diffusion in the boundary layer allow near-wall air to recover part of the kinetic-energy temperature rise. We therefore introduce the recovery factor $r_T$:
\[
 c_p(T_{\rm aw}-T)=r_T\frac{v^2}{2}.
\]
The Prandtl number $\Pr$, the ratio of momentum diffusivity to thermal diffusivity, characterizes the relative strengths of these transport processes. According to a review of convective heat transfer~\cite[p.~58]{thermal}, the recovery factor for a turbulent boundary layer can be approximated by $r_T=\Pr^{1/3}$. Taking $\Pr=0.71$ under the constant-property approximation for air yields $r_T=0.8921$. The recovery-temperature profile is thus
\begin{equation}
 T_{\rm aw}(h)=T(h)+r_T\frac{v(h)^2}{2c_p}.
 \label{eq:heat}
\end{equation}
Substituting the speed profile from Eq.~\eqref{eq:vh} into Eq.~\eqref{eq:heat} determines the recovery-temperature profile. Throughout this paper, $T_{\rm aw}$ denotes the adiabatic-wall reference temperature of the aerodynamic environment.

\subsection{Attitude Dynamics}\label{sec:attitude}
We approximate the mass distribution of the body and equipment by a uniform solid cylinder of length $L$ and radius $r$. As described in Section~1.2, its axis follows the longitudinal axis of the body and lies in the horizontal plane, perpendicular to the vertical $z$ axis. The geometry and angular coordinates are illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:geometry}. We distinguish two rotational degrees of freedom: the pitch angle $\theta$ measures rotation about the $y$ axis from the horizontal reference posture, and the flat-spin angle $\psi$ measures rotation about the $z$ axis.

\par
Both reference rotation axes are perpendicular to the cylinder axis. The moment of inertia about the vertical axis is
\[
 I_z=\frac{m}{12}(L^2+3r^2).
\]
In the reference posture, the pitch moment of inertia equals $I_z$. Using representative human dimensions $L=1.9$\,m and $r=0.3$\,m yields $I_z=\CylinderInertia$\,kg m$^2$. The distance from the head to the flat-spin axis is taken as $r_h=0.5$\,m.

Pitch changes the angle between the body axis and the main airflow, thereby altering the windward pressure distribution. We assume that the reference posture is stable in pitch, so this change produces a restoring effect. Flat spin primarily changes the azimuth about the main airflow direction; approximately uniform vertical inflow does not provide the same angle-of-attack restoring mechanism, and persistent circumferential asymmetry can increase the spin rate. We therefore neglect the suppressed pitch perturbation and focus on flat spin.

Gravity acts through the center of mass and produces no moment about it. Following the treatment of yawing moments in the aerodynamics literature~\cite[p.~39]{nguyen}, we separate the flat-spin torque into a driving bias term and a resisting damping term.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Bias torque.} During rapid descent, the airflow is deflected and decelerated over the body and equipment, producing pressure and tangential forces. Asymmetry in shape or posture can leave a net torque:
\[
 \tau_{\rm bias}=\frac12\rho v^2ALC_n.
\]
The positive direction is chosen to match the bias-driven rotation.
\item \textbf{Rotational damping.} Once the body rotates, a surface point at distance $s$ from the axis acquires an additional tangential speed $\omega s$. This changes the direction and magnitude of the local relative airflow. The resulting force change produces an opposing torque. For $|\omega|L/(2v)\ll1$, the dependence of aerodynamic torque on angular speed is retained to first order:
\[
 \tau_{\rm damp}=-\frac14\rho vAL^2C_{n\omega}\omega.
\]

\end{enumerate}

The parameters in these expressions are specified as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Descent speed.} Because the attitude-response timescale is much shorter than the free-fall timescale, $v$ is treated as approximately constant.
\item \textbf{Area.} The value of $A$ is the same as in Section~4.2.1.
\item \textbf{Bias coefficient.} Guided by the order of magnitude of yawing-moment coefficients in flight-test literature~\cite[p.~6]{cobleigh}, we take $C_n=0.05$.
\item \textbf{Damping coefficient.} Using yaw-damping derivatives from an aircraft simulation study~\cite[p.~81]{nguyen} as a reference, we take the positive damping coefficient $C_{n\omega}=1$.
\end{enumerate}

Applying angular-momentum balance about the vertical axis through the center of mass yields a first-order linear equation for the flat-spin angular speed:
\begin{equation*}
 I_z\frac{d\omega}{d\tau}+\frac14\rho vAL^2C_{n\omega}\omega
 =\frac12\rho v^2ALC_n.
\end{equation*}
The analytical solution is
\begin{equation}
 \omega(\tau)=\frac{2vC_n}{LC_{n\omega}}
 \left[1-\exp\left(-\frac{\rho vAL^2C_{n\omega}}{4I_z}\tau\right)\right].
 \label{eq:spinresponse}
\end{equation}
This exponential response reflects the competition between bias and damping. Substituting the local $\rho(h)$ and $v(h)$ at each altitude allows the rotational response over the same local time interval to be compared along the descent.

\subsection{Parachute Descent}
\subsubsection{Inflation}\label{sec:inflation}
After deployment, the packed canopy gradually inflates. Its effective area grows, increasing drag, while the falling speed decreases and reduces the quadratic speed factor in the drag. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:canopyforces}, the forces are as follows:
\par
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[>=Stealth,thick,font=\figurefont\fontsize{11bp}{13bp}\selectfont]
 \draw[fill=blue!10] (-.55,.1) ellipse [x radius=.8,y radius=.25];
 \fill (-.55,.1) circle (2pt) node[below right] {\figvar{O}};
 \draw[->,blue!70!black] (-.85,.1)--(-.85,1.7) node[above] {\figvar{D}};
 \draw[->,green!45!black] (-.25,.1)--(-.25,2.2) node[above] {\figvar{D}\textsubscript{c}};
 \draw[->,red!70!black] (-.55,.1)--(-.55,-1.5) node[below] {\figvar{mg}(\figvar{h})};
 \draw[->] (1.2,.8)--(1.2,-.9) node[midway,right] {\figvar{v}};

\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{Forces during and after parachute inflation; $D_c$ develops as the canopy fills.}\label{fig:canopyforces}
\end{figure}
\par
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Gravity.} The downward force has magnitude $mg(h)$. The mass remains 190\,kg, and gravitational acceleration follows Eq.~\eqref{eq:gravity}.
\item \textbf{Body drag.} Equation~\eqref{eq:force} applies, with $A$ equal to the calibrated effective area.
\item \textbf{Canopy drag.} This force acts upward, with magnitude
\begin{equation}
 D_c=\frac12\rho(h)v^2B_cS(u).
 \label{eq:canopyforce}
\end{equation}
The parameters and their basis are specified below.
\begin{enumerate}
\renewcommand{\labelenumii}{\arabic{enumii}.}
\item \textbf{Parachute drag area.} The fully inflated drag area is $B_c=C_{D,c}A_c$, where $A_c$ is the equivalent canopy reference area and $C_{D,c}$ is its drag coefficient. We take $C_{D,c}=1.5$. In Section~\ref{sec:canopycalibration}, $B_c$ is calibrated to the sea-level descent speed, after which $A_c=B_c/C_{D,c}$.
\item \textbf{Inflation fraction.} Let $t_o$ denote the deployment time and $\tau_c$ the inflation duration. With normalized inflation time $u=(t-t_o)/\tau_c$, $S(u)$ is the ratio of the instantaneous effective canopy drag area to its fully inflated value. We assume that this ratio starts at zero and reaches one at full inflation, with zero area-growth rate at both endpoints: $S(0)=0$, $S(1)=1$, $S'(0)=0$, and $S'(1)=0$. For $0<u<1$, the lowest-degree polynomial satisfying these four independent conditions yields
\begin{equation*}
 S(u)=\begin{cases}0,&u\le0,\\3u^2-2u^3,&0<u<1,\\1,&u\ge1.\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
Performance specifications from a parachute manufacturer describe a soft main-canopy opening over approximately 6\,s to reduce opening shock. We adopt a slightly longer inflation duration, $\tau_c=8$\,s, as a design assumption and assess this choice using the calculated peak load in Section~6.4.
\item \textbf{Air density.} Equation~\eqref{eq:density} continues to apply, allowing density to vary with altitude during inflation.
\end{enumerate}

\end{enumerate}

Applying Newton\textquotesingle s second law to the complete system yields
\[
 m\frac{\mathrm d^2h}{\mathrm dt^2}=-mg(h)+D+D_c.
\]
Using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:force} and \eqref{eq:canopyforce}, we obtain
\begin{equation}
 v\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dh}=-g(h)+\frac{\rho(h)v^2}{2m}\left[C_D(M)A+B_cS(u)\right].
 \label{eq:canopy}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Fully Inflated Descent and Landing}\label{sec:landingmodel}
Once the canopy is fully inflated, $S=1$. With $C_D=0.6$ at low speed, Eq.~\eqref{eq:canopy} reduces to
\begin{equation}
 v\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dh}=-g(h)+\frac{\rho(h)(0.6A+B_c)}{2m}v^2.
 \label{eq:postcanopy}
\end{equation}
The descent speed therefore tends toward the local equilibrium speed:
\begin{equation}
 v_{t,c}(h)=\sqrt{\frac{2mg(h)}{\rho(h)(0.6A+B_c)}}.
 \label{eq:canopyterminal}
\end{equation}
The values of $A$, $B_c$, and the gas properties are unchanged from the inflation stage. As altitude decreases, density increases and the equilibrium speed slowly falls; the actual descent speed gradually follows this variation. The landing speed $v_{\rm land}$ is therefore approximately $v_{t,c}(0)$.

\section{Model Calibration and Results}
\subsection{Calibration and Model Checks}
Two parameters in the preceding models remain to be determined: the effective body frontal area $A$ and the parachute drag area $B_c$. Here we use reference data to calibrate both parameters and to assess the representation of the atmosphere and the descent process.

\subsubsection{Atmosphere Check}
We select records for stations 72364 and 72365 from the public upper-air sounding archive for the two stations to assess the temperature model in Eq.~\eqref{eq:temperature} and the density model in Eq.~\eqref{eq:density}. The reference data comprise eight soundings: both stations at 12 UTC on March 15, July 25, and October 14, 2012, and at 00 UTC on October 15. Observed density is calculated from measured pressure and temperature as $p/(RT)$. Geopotential altitude is converted to geometric altitude using Eq.~\eqref{eq:height} before comparison.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:atmcheck}, the October temperature and density profiles at both stations broadly follow the mean vertical structure of the standard atmosphere. Across all eight soundings at geopotential altitudes of 10, 20, and 25\,km, the maximum absolute relative density deviation is $8.66\%$.
\paperfig{raw_q1_atmosphere}{Comparison of the standard atmosphere with two soundings at 12 UTC on October 14, 2012.}{fig:atmcheck}

\subsubsection{Parachute Drag Area}\label{sec:canopycalibration}
According to a parachute manufacturer\textquotesingle s performance specifications, the reference main canopy achieves a sea-level descent speed below 18\,ft/s, approximately 5.49\,m/s, at a total load of about 181\,kg. Using this speed as a reference, we choose $v_d=5.5$\,m/s as the design sea-level equilibrium speed for the specified 190\,kg system and determine the required parachute drag area.

Setting $h=0$ and $v_{t,c}(0)=v_d$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:canopyterminal} and rearranging yields
\[
 B_c=\frac{2mg_0}{\rho(0)v_d^2}-0.6A.
\]
With sea-level density $\rho(0)=1.225$\,kg/m$^3$ and $A=\FitArea$\,m$^2$ calibrated from the three peak speeds in the next subsection, we obtain $B_c=\CanopyB$\,m$^2$.

\subsubsection{Joint Frontal-Area Calibration}\label{sec:calibration}
In 2012, Austrian space diver Felix Baumgartner ascended in a high-altitude balloon capsule and descended in a pressure suit. He completed two space-diving tests on March 15 and July 25, followed by the program\textquotesingle s main space dive on October 14. We compile the release altitudes and peak speeds of all three dives from the mission report~\cite{summit}. These observations describe the speed response from different initial altitudes, allowing the unknown effective frontal area $A$ to be jointly determined once the drag-coefficient relation is specified.

Following the mass used in the aerodynamic study~\cite{guerster}, we take $m_{\rm ref}=121.2$\,kg. Each dive starts from its own release altitude with $v(0)=0$. We integrate the equivalent time-domain form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:vh}, together with Eqs.~\eqref{eq:density} and \eqref{eq:drag}, down to the deployment altitude and extract the maximum speed of each trajectory, $v_{\max,i}^{\rm model}(A)$. We then minimize the sum of squared relative peak-speed residuals:
\begin{equation*}
 A^*=\underset{0.3\le A\le3.0\;\mathrm{m^2}}{\arg\min}
 \sum_{i=1}^{3}\left[
 \frac{v_{\max,i}^{\rm model}(A)}{v_{\max,i}^{\rm obs}}-1\right]^2.
\end{equation*}
The fitted area is $A^*=\FitArea$\,m$^2$.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:flights}, the fitted peak speeds agree closely with all three observations.

\paperfig{raw_q1_flights}{Observed and jointly fitted peak speeds, with relative errors for the three dives.}{fig:flights}

\subsubsection{October Speed Profile}
During the main space dive on October 14, 2012, an onboard GPS unit recorded the descent trajectory. An aerodynamic study~\cite{guerster} used these measurements to analyze human-body aerodynamics; its speed--altitude curve provides an observational basis for comparing the full descent. We compare the simulated profile with this measured curve to assess whether calibration using peak speeds also captures the complete motion.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:october}, the simulated and observed curves exhibit similar acceleration and deceleration trends.
\paperfig{result_q1_validation}{Simulated October speed--altitude profile compared with digitized values from the published measured curve.}{fig:october}

\subsection{Descent Characteristics}
For the 190\,kg system, we consider release from rest at $h_0=40,50,60,70,80$\,km. The descent trajectories follow the equivalent time-domain form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:vh}; drag and recovery temperature then follow Eqs.~\eqref{eq:force} and \eqref{eq:heat}. The predicted speed, drag, and temperature profiles are shown in Figures~\ref{fig:velocity}, \ref{fig:forcefamily}, and \ref{fig:tempfamily}, respectively. They reveal the following three physical features.

\subsubsection{Transonic Drag Rise}
As seen in Figure~\ref{fig:velocity}, a higher release altitude provides a longer acceleration distance in thin air and generally produces a higher peak speed. Near the speed of sound, the drag coefficient in Eq.~\eqref{eq:drag} rises from its low-speed plateau to a transonic peak. This is the additional drag associated with compressibility, commonly described as the sound-barrier effect.
\paperfig{family_velocity}{Speed--altitude profiles for different release altitudes; dots mark locations near $M=1$.}{fig:velocity}

\paperfig{family_force}{Aerodynamic drag--altitude profiles for different release altitudes; dots mark locations near $M=1$.}{fig:forcefamily}

\subsubsection{Terminal-Speed Convergence}
The local terminal speed in the reference posture follows from force balance:
\begin{equation*}
 \frac12\rho(h)C_D(v_t/c)Av_t^2=mg(h).
\end{equation*}
In the low-altitude subsonic region, $C_D=0.6$, so
\begin{equation}
 v_t(h)=\sqrt{\frac{2mg(h)}{0.6\rho(h)A}}.
 \label{eq:terminal}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:terminal} shows that, for specified mass, effective area, and local atmospheric conditions, terminal speed does not depend on release altitude $h_0$. The calculated values are \TerminalGround\,m/s at sea level and \TerminalFive\,m/s at 5\,km.

This behavior is also apparent in Figure~\ref{fig:terminal}: the low-altitude speed profiles from releases at 40--80\,km gradually converge toward the same local equilibrium curve.
\paperfig{terminal_convergence}{Speed profiles and local free-fall terminal speed over 0--20\,km. Trajectories from different release altitudes converge during descent and end at parachute deployment.}{fig:terminal}

\subsubsection{Recovery-Temperature Profile}
As seen in Figure~\ref{fig:tempfamily}, recovery temperature first rises markedly during acceleration and then falls as the diver decelerates at lower altitudes.
\paperfig{family_temperature}{Recovery-temperature profiles for different release altitudes.}{fig:tempfamily}

When speed reaches its maximum, the ambient atmospheric temperature may continue to change during descent. Equation~\eqref{eq:heat} therefore implies that the recovery-temperature peak need not coincide with the speed peak.

\section{Hazard Assessment}
Section~5 shows that a higher release altitude generally leads to a higher peak speed and greater aerodynamic heating. Entry into denser air also produces substantial drag and aerodynamic torque. The main hazards during descent can therefore be grouped into free-fall loads, high temperature, and rotation. Meanwhile, low-altitude speed convergence makes the state at parachute deployment insensitive to sufficiently high release altitudes, and the equilibrium speed under a fully inflated canopy depends primarily on local density and canopy parameters. We first examine the three main hazards, then use the low-altitude behavior to assess the selected deployment and landing scheme.

\subsection{Free-Fall Loads}
Drag decelerates the body while imposing nongravitational loads on the diver and equipment. The free-fall hazard is therefore assessed using aerodynamic specific force. Define
\begin{equation*}
 n_D(h)=\frac{D(h)}{mg_0}.
\end{equation*}
We adopt the peak-load criterion
\begin{equation}
 \max_{\text{free fall}}n_D\le\FreeLimit.
 \label{eq:freelimit}
\end{equation}
The net acceleration $\dot v=g-D/m$ differs from the aerodynamic specific force $D/m$ experienced by the body. In particular, the latter is small during the initial near-ballistic fall but may exceed $g_0$ during deceleration.

In a belly-down posture, the dominant aerodynamic force acts approximately along the chest--back direction, so the human $G_x$ direction in a crewed-spaceflight standard provides an order-of-magnitude reference. The standard~\cite[Sec.~6.5.1, Fig.~4]{nasastd} indicates that load limits depend on duration and physiological condition. We choose a load-factor threshold of 5 for this model.

We calculate the free-fall load using Eq.~\eqref{eq:vh} and determine its altitude boundary using Eq.~\eqref{eq:freelimit}. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:riskfree}, the peak load rises with release altitude. Over 0.1--86\,km, its maximum is \ScanFreeMax\,$g_0$, below the design limit. To estimate the individual upper altitude for this criterion, only this subsection continuously extrapolates the temperature gradient, hydrostatic pressure, and density relations of the final atmospheric layer to a geometric altitude of 110\,km. The resulting free-fall load boundary is \LoadHeight\,km. In this extrapolated case, the peak of \FreeLimit\,$g_0$ occurs \LoadPeakTime\,s after release at a geometric altitude of \LoadPeakHeight\,km.
\paperfig{risk_free}{Peak free-fall load and screening limit. Shading and the dashed curve above 86\,km denote the atmospheric extrapolation used only in this subsection.}{fig:riskfree}

\subsection{Thermal Hazard}
Aerodynamic heating may affect the outer suit, visor, and seals. The quantity calculated here is the recovery temperature in Eq.~\eqref{eq:heat}, for which we impose the screening condition
\begin{equation*}
 \max_{\text{free fall}}T_{\rm aw}(h)\le T_{\rm lim}.
\end{equation*}
Published spacesuit environmental information describes temperatures during a spacewalk ranging from about $-250\,{}^\circ\mathrm F$ to about $250\,{}^\circ\mathrm F$ in sunlight, or approximately 116--394\,K. Using the upper end of this environmental range as a reference and rounding downward, we select $T_{\rm lim}=\HeatLimit$\,K as the recovery-temperature screening value.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:riskheat}, peak recovery temperature increases with release altitude and intersects the screening limit at \MaxHeight\,km. In this limiting case, the recovery temperature reaches \HeatLimit\,K at \HeatPeakTime\,s after release and a geometric altitude of \HeatPeakHeight\,km.
\paperfig{risk_heat}{Peak recovery temperature versus release altitude. The intersection identifies the altitude boundary for the selected thermal design condition.}{fig:riskheat}

\subsection{Flat-Spin Hazard}
Flat spin subjects the head to radial acceleration, potentially affecting blood distribution and attitude control. The linear damping model in Section~4 describes local perturbations whose rotational speed is small relative to the main airflow. The diver must therefore detect the spin promptly and adjust posture to reduce aerodynamic asymmetry. We assume a total of $\SpinResponseTime$\,s from the onset of the bias through detection to completion of the adjustment.
Consistent with Section~6.1, we select $\SpinLimit\,g_0$ as the head radial-acceleration threshold for this brief response. With $r_h=0.5$\,m and Eq.~\eqref{eq:spinresponse}, we calculate the following separately at every position along the trajectory:
\[
 n_r(h)=\frac{r_h[\omega(1\,\mathrm s;h)]^2}{g_0}.
\]
The entire free-fall segment must satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \max_{\text{free fall}} n_r(h)\le\SpinLimit.
 \label{eq:spinlimit}
\end{equation}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:riskspin}, the maximum local 1\,s radial acceleration increases with release altitude and reaches the limit in Eq.~\eqref{eq:spinlimit} at $h_0=\SpinHeight$\,km.
\paperfig{risk_spin}{Maximum local 1\,s radial acceleration versus release altitude and the flat-spin screening boundary.}{fig:riskspin}

\subsection{Parachute-Opening Loads}
Opening loads are transmitted to the body through the harness and are measured by the total aerodynamic specific force:
\[
 n_o=\frac{D+D_c}{mg_0}.
\]
We use the same threshold as in Section~6.1:
\begin{equation}
 \max_{\text{inflation and canopy descent}}n_o\le\OpenLimit.
 \label{eq:openinglimit}
\end{equation}

The low-altitude convergence in Section~5 implies that, at a fixed deployment altitude and with fixed canopy parameters, the deployment states from sufficiently high releases approach one another. Satisfaction of Eq.~\eqref{eq:openinglimit} therefore depends primarily on the selected low-altitude deployment state and canopy parameters. For releases at 40--80\,km, the spread in deployment speed is less than \OpenSpread\,m/s, and the spread in peak opening load is less than \OpenLoadSpread\,$g_0$. These differences are very small.

Following the deployment altitude in the flight study and mission records~\cite{guerster,summit}, we take $h_o=2567$\,m and apply Eq.~\eqref{eq:canopy}. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:opening}, the peak opening load is \OpenG\,$g_0$, below the threshold. This low-altitude deployment scheme therefore does not limit the release altitude.
\paperfig{process_q1_opening}{Load and speed during the selected 8\,s inflation, using the deployment state at the combined altitude boundary.}{fig:opening}

\subsection{Landing}
We assess landing using the vertical speed immediately before ground contact. With reference to sea-level descent speeds in parachute manufacturer specifications, we adopt
\begin{equation*}
 v_{\rm land}\le\LandingLimit\;\mathrm{m/s}.
\end{equation*}

As in Section~6.4, the local equilibrium speed under a fully inflated canopy is essentially independent of release altitude. Keeping the 2567\,m deployment altitude, Eq.~\eqref{eq:postcanopy} yields a landing speed of \LandingV\,m/s at the combined boundary, below 6\,m/s. Landing speed therefore imposes no maximum release altitude.

\section{Model Evaluation}
\subsection{Strengths}
The model connects the standard atmosphere, translation, aerodynamic heating indicator, attitude, and parachute descent through force balance, energy conservation, and angular-momentum balance. Calibration and prediction use the same Mach-dependent drag relation. Fitting three peak speeds and comparing the full October profile provide evidence at different levels of detail.
\subsection{Limitations}
A single effective area and a common reference mass cannot fully distinguish differences in posture and equipment among the three dives. October data are used in peak-speed calibration, and the drag coefficient is derived from the same flight, so the profile comparison is not an independent predictive validation. The constant extrapolation of drag for $M>1.25$ lies outside the original empirical range, whereas the central case reaches a peak Mach number of \PeakMach. This limits confidence in the high-altitude predictions.

The cylindrical inertia and torque coefficients are simplified design assumptions, and transferring the order of magnitude of aircraft aerodynamic derivatives to the human body lacks direct human measurements. Neglecting pitch requires a stable reference posture and small perturbations. The local flat-spin solution freezes density and speed and assumes that adjustment is completed within 1\,s of bias onset; it does not represent cumulative rotation or the complete active-control process. Recovery temperature remains an aerodynamic-environment indicator. The \HeatLimit\,K, load, and landing screening values require further correspondence with specific equipment and human conditions.
